What is a worldview?

A worldview is built from the beliefs you hold about how the world works and should be governed — beliefs that already have names and long histories behind them, known as isms. Isms also typically cover your values, spiritual choices and approach to finding truth about life. You may not think of your own views this way, but they almost always trace back to isms already studied and written about extensively.

This app draws on 47 of the most widely known isms — not the complete list that exists, but the ones most people have encountered, each with a direct or indirect bearing on governance or the control of people. The isms you personally endorse make up your worldview. Naming them gives you a clearer picture of how you see life, your community, and your government, whether or not you think of yourself as politically or spiritually engaged.

Building it is automatic: select the isms you agree with in the Encyclopedia (Step 1), and they populate your Worldview list (Step 2). Step 3 then adds relevant narrative from the book A Critique of Isms — pros, cons, and further commentary from the author — producing a customized worldview you can print or share.

The Author's Vision

This isn't a blueprint — it's a vision, and an honest one, because the ideas it rests on don't naturally get along. Throughout history, socialists and capitalists, feminists and traditionalists, environmentalists and growth-driven markets have been in genuine tension. What I'm proposing isn't that one of these wins, but that seven of them hold each other in check at the same time.

I call these the 7 pillars: Secular Humanism, Scientism, Capitalism and Socialism paired together as “Capsolism,” Environmentalism, Feminism, Liberalism, and a Reimagined Democracy. Secular Humanism is the foundation — a commitment to human dignity and welfare that every other pillar must answer to. Scientism means decisions guided by evidence, not ideology or mysticism. Capsolism lets markets drive innovation while regulation and a strong welfare state keep profit from coming at the cost of workers, communities, or the planet. Environmentalism keeps all of it inside hard ecological limits. Feminism means equity built into institutions from the start, not added later. Liberalism protects individual rights and constitutional limits on power. And a Reimagined Democracy — one built for an era of misinformation and demagogues — is the mechanism that makes the other six accountable to the people they're meant to serve.

Remove any one pillar and the others start to fail: capitalism without environmentalism pushes growth past safe limits; democracy without feminism reproduces old hierarchies; science without humanism reduces people to data points. None of this guarantees a better world. But it's the closest thing to a stable foundation history has offered so far — and it's worth the attempt.

The 7 Pillars

A quick reference — see "The Author's Vision" on Home for the fuller argument.

1. Secular Humanism — The foundation. A commitment to human dignity, welfare, and reason, free of religious doctrine, that every other pillar must answer to.
2. Scientism — Decisions guided by evidence and the scientific method, not ideology, mysticism, or guesswork.
3. Capitalism & Socialism (“Capsolism”) — Markets drive innovation and growth, but regulation and a strong welfare state keep profit from coming at the cost of workers, communities, or the planet.
4. Environmentalism — Growth and prosperity operate within hard ecological limits, not despite them.
5. Feminism — Gender equity built into every institution from the start, not added afterward.
6. Liberalism — Individual rights and constitutional limits on power, protecting people from the excesses of both markets and the state.
7. Reimagined Democracy — A voting and governance system redesigned for an era of misinformation and demagogues, holding the other six pillars accountable to the people they serve.
